# Ethanol Raman spectrum - absolute wavenumber

 ## 1. Exploration of the Original Ethanol Spectrum

The original ethanol Raman spectrum is first explored to understand the
structure of the CSV file before applying any processing.

The dataset contains the Raman spectrum and an additional section called
`Peaks`. The spectrum is represented by the index of the DataFrame and
the intensity values stored in the `Unnamed: 0` column.

During the initial exploration, the following steps are performed:

* Load the original ethanol Raman spectrum.
* Inspect the structure and data types of the DataFrame.
* Identify non-numeric values in the index.
* Locate the `Peaks` section.
* Inspect the data before and after the `Peaks` label.
* Plot the original data to understand how the spectrum and peak
  information are stored.

This exploratory step is important because the peak information is stored
after the original Raman spectrum in the same CSV file. The original data
are kept unchanged throughout the analysis.


## 2. Create a Clean Raman Spectrum

The original ethanol CSV file contains the Raman spectrum followed by a
separate section called `Peaks`.

The `Peaks` section contains information about detected peaks and is not
part of the original spectrum.

A new DataFrame called `etanol_clean` is created using only the spectral
data. The original DataFrame and CSV file are not modified.

The new DataFrame contains two columns:

- `Wavenumber_cm-1`: absolute wavenumber in cm⁻¹.
- `Intensity`: Raman intensity.


## 3. Extract the Peak Information

The original ethanol CSV file contains a `Peaks` section after the
Raman spectrum.

This section contains the position and intensity of the detected peaks.
The peak information is extracted into a new DataFrame called
`etanol_peaks`.

The original DataFrame is not modified.






Alcohols: ethanol, isopropanol, and methanol.—We initially observed that the choice
of the incident beam wavelengths had no significant impact on the Raman spectra of these
alcohol fluids. Therefore, only data taken at 532 nm are shown in Figure 2, which compares
the Raman spectra for three pure alcohols: ethanol, methanol, and isopropanol (IPA).
The ethanol spectrum has a strong band at a Raman shift of 880 cm−1, because of
carbon-carbon (C–C) bond symmetric stretching in the molecule, and two small bands at
1,040 and 1,090 cm−1, corresponding, respectively, to carbon–oxygen (C–O) bond stretching
andCH3 rocking or to C–C–O bonds asymmetric vibrations (Boyaci et al. 2012, Pappas
et al. 2016). Very strong peaks are observed at 2,880, 2,930, and 2,970 cm−1, corresponding
to the C–H bond stretching modes, whereas the broad band around 3,350 cm−1 is assigned
to the OH bond stretching mode (Numata et al. 2011). Some additional peaks are present
in the spectral fingerprint at 430 (C–O deformation), 1,270 (CH2 twisting), 1,450 (CH2 scissoring),
1,480 (CH3 asymmetric deformation), and 2,710 cm−1 (Picard et al. 2007, Pappas
et al. 2016).
Isopropyl alcohol and ethanol have relatively similar spectral fingerprints, which was expected,
because these twomolecules are chemically close (Jin et al. 2018). Yet some features
make it possible to differentiate thesemolecules. First, there is a significant shift of the C–C
stretching peak from 880 cm−1 for ethanol to 820 cm−1 for IPA. Second, the C–H stretching
peak of ethanol (2,930 cm−1) splits into two peaks for IPA (2,920 and 2,940 cm−1).
The Raman spectrum of methanol contains fewer peaks, which is logical, because it is
the simplest of the alcohols. For this fluid, single C–O stretching and CH3 rocking peaks
are observed, respectively, at 1,040 and 1,110 cm−1. Moreover, the CH3 asymmetric deformation
peak and the three C–H symmetric stretching peaks observed, respectively, at 1,455,
2,885, 2,930, and 2,980 cm−1 for ethanol, are shifted to two peaks, only situated at 1,480
and 2,945 cm−1 (Vaskova 2014).
